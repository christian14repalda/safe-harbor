Understanding VAWC

What the law means by violence against women and their children, and the myths that keep people silent.

What is VAWC?

VAWC stands for violence against women and their children. Under RA 9262, it means any act or series of acts that causes, or is likely to cause, physical, sexual or psychological harm, or economic abuse. This includes threats, battery, assault, coercion, harassment and arbitrarily taking away a woman's freedom.[1]

Who is covered

The law protects a woman who is, or used to be, in any of these situations with the person who harms her:[1]

  • Married to him, or formerly married to him
  • In a sexual or dating relationship with him, now or in the past
  • Has a child with him

It also protects her children, whether they are legitimate or not.[1] This means a boyfriend, an ex-boyfriend and a live-in partner can all be covered. A marriage certificate is not needed.

Four forms of abuse

These are plain-language examples based on Sections 3 and 5 of RA 9262.[1]

Physical
Hitting, pushing, choking or any act that causes bodily harm, and threats to do these things.
Sexual
Forcing sex or sexual acts, or using threats or pressure to make a woman do something sexual that she does not want to do.
Psychological
Repeated insults and humiliation, intimidation, stalking, harassment, controlling where she goes, and making her or her children witness abuse.
Economic
Withholding financial support, stopping her from working or earning, and controlling or taking her money or property.

Children are affected too, including when they only see or hear the abuse. RA 7610 also protects children against abuse, exploitation and discrimination in general.[1][10]

Myths and facts

Tap a myth to read the fact.

Myth: What happens between a couple is a private family matter.

Fact: RA 9262 treats violence against women and children as a public crime. Any citizen with personal knowledge of what happened may start the complaint.[1]

Myth: It is only abuse if there are bruises.

Fact: The law also covers psychological, sexual and economic abuse. Threats, stalking, constant humiliation and controlling money are all covered.[1]

Myth: It only counts if the couple is married.

Fact: Dating partners, live-in partners, ex-partners and the other parent of her child are covered.[1]

Myth: The barangay should get the couple to settle and make peace.

Fact: The law forbids barangay officials and the court from pressuring a woman who applies for a protection order to compromise or give up the relief she is asking for.[1]

Myth: If it were really that bad, she would just leave.

Fact: Fear, threats, money problems and worry about the children can all make leaving very hard. The responsibility for the abuse belongs to the person who commits it, not to the person who is hurt.
